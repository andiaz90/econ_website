abstract: "The U.S. teen birth rate has fallen sharply since the early 1990s, and lower birth rates before age 20 account for a large share of the decline in total fertility over the same period. Measured by birth cohort, early first births first rose and then fell, and the underlying forces were partly offsetting: family backgrounds improved throughout, while early births within family backgrounds first rose and then fell. I estimate a life-cycle model of schooling, work, marriage and contraceptive use on three cohorts of women, each measured with the same surveys, and decompose the change in early childbearing into the value of avoiding a birth, the cost and efficacy of contraception, exposure, abortion, the marriage market, the safety net and family background. Less sexual activity, more effective methods and a lower cost of contraception account for about 60 percent of the fall in births at ages 15–19 between women born in 1976–80 and 1992–96, better earnings and schooling prospects for a fifth, and better-educated mothers for a seventh. Among the cohorts who were teenagers in the 1990s, more sexual activity raised early births about as much as better-educated mothers lowered them."
